### How to Stay Current in Bioinformatics/Genomics

A few folks have asked me how I get my news and stay on top of what's going on in my field, so I thought I'd share my strategy. With so many sources of information begging for your attention, the difficulty is not necessarily finding what's interesting, but filtering out what isn't. What you *don't* read is just as important as what you do, so when it comes to things like RSS, Twitter, and especially e-mail, it's essential to filter out sources where the content consistently fails to be relevant or capture your interest. I run a bioinformatics core, so I'm more broadly interested in applied methodology and study design rather than any particular phenotype, model system, disease, or method. With that in mind, here's how I stay current with things that are relevant to me. Please leave comments with what you're reading and what you find useful that I omitted here.

**RSS**

I get the majority of my news from [RSS feeds](http://support.google.com/reader/bin/answer.py?hl=en&answer=113517) from blogs and journals in my field. I spend about 15 minutes per day going through headlines from the following sources:

*Journals*. Most journals have separate RSS feeds for their current table of contents as well as their advance online ahead-of-print articles.

- [Bioinformatics - RSS feed of current and advance online publications](http://bioinformatics.oxfordjournals.org/rss/)
- [Genome Research - current & advance](http://genome.cshlp.org/rss/)
- [Genome Biology - editors picks, latest, most viewed, most forwarded](http://genomebiology.com/). (Hit the RSS icon under each tab).
- [PLoS Genetics - new articles](http://www.plosgenetics.org/static/rssFeeds.action)
- [PLoS Computational Biology - new articles](http://www.ploscompbiol.org/static/rssFeeds.action)
- [Nature Genetics - current TOC and AOP](http://www.nature.com/ng/newsfeeds.html)
- [Nature Reviews Genetics - current TOC and AOP](http://www.nature.com/nrg/info/newsfeeds.html)

*Blogs*. Some of these blogs are very relevant to what I do on the job. Others are more personal interest.

- [The OpenHelix Blog](http://blog.openhelix.eu/)
- [Ensembl blog](http://www.ensembl.info/)
- [Galaxy News](http://wiki.g2.bx.psu.edu/News)
- [Blue Collar Bioinformatics](http://bcbio.wordpress.com/)
- [Homologus](http://www.homolog.us/blogs/)
- [Golden Helix - our 2 SNPs](http://blog.goldenhelix.com/)
- [Genomics Law Report](http://genomicslawreport.com/)
- [R-bloggers](http://www.r-bloggers.com/) (aggregates feeds from \>350 blogs about R)
- [Genomes Unzipped](http://genomesunzipped.org/)
- [Jason Moore's Epistasis Blog](http://compgen.blogspot.com/)
- [23andMe - the Spitoon](http://spittoon.23andme.com/)

*Forums.*

- [Seqanswers - bioinformatics forum](http://seqanswers.com/forums/forumdisplay.php?f=18)
- [Seqanswers - RNA-Seq forum](http://seqanswers.com/forums/forumdisplay.php?f=26)
- [BioStar](http://www.biostars.org/rss/)

**Mailing lists**\
**  **
I prefer to keep work and personal email separate, but I have all my mailing list email sent to my Gmail because Gmail's search is better than any alternative. I have a filter set up to automatically filter and tag mailing list digests under a "Work" label so I can get to them (or filter them from my inbox) easily.

- [Bioconductor](http://www.bioconductor.org/help/mailing-list/) (daily digest)
- [Galaxy mailing lists](http://wiki.g2.bx.psu.edu/Mailing%20Lists). I subscribe to the -announce, -user, and -dev mailing lists, but I have a Gmail filter set up to automatically skip the inbox and mark read messages from the -user and -dev lists. I don't care to look at these every day, but again, it's handy to be able to use Gmail's search functionality to look through old mailing list responses.

**Email Alerts & Subscriptions**

Again, email can get out of hand sometimes, so I prefer to only have things that I really don't want to miss sent to my email. The rest I use RSS.

- [SeqAnswers](http://seqanswers.com/) subscriptions. When I ask a question or find a question that's relevant to something I'm working on, I subscribe to that thread for email alerts whenever a new response is posted. 
- [Google Scholar alerts](http://scholar.google.com/). I have alerts set up to send me emails based on certain topics (e.g. \[ rna-seq \| transcriptome sequencing \| RNA-sequencing \] or \[ intitle:"chip-seq" \]), or when certain people publish (e.g. \["ritchie md" & vanderbilt\]). I also use this to alert me when certain consortia publish (e.g. \["Population Architecture using Genomics and Epidemiology"\]).
- [PubMed Saved Searches using MyNCBI](http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sites/myncbi/), because Google Scholar doesn't catch everything. I have alerts set up for RNA-seq, ChIP-Seq, bioinformatics methods, etc.
- [GenomeWeb subscriptions](http://www.genomeweb.com/). Most of these are once per week, except Daily Scan. I subscribe to Daily Scan, Genome Technology, BioInform, Clinical Sequencing News, In Sequence, and Pharmacogenomics Reporter. BioInform has a "Bioinformatics Papers of Note", and In Sequence has a "Sequencing papers of note" column in every issue. These are good for catching things I might have missed with the Scholar and Pubmed alerts.

**Twitter**

99.9% of Twitter users have way too much time on their hands, but when used effectively, Twitter can be incredibly powerful for both consuming and contributing to the dialogue in your field. Twitter can be an excellent real-time source of new publications, fresh developments, and current opinion, but it can also quickly become a time sink. I can tolerate an occasional Friday afternoon humorous digression, but as soon as off-topic tweets become regular it's time to unfollow. The same is true with groups/companies - some deliver interesting and broadly applicable content (e.g. 23andMe), while others are purely a failed attempt at marketing while not offering any substantive value to their followers. A good place to start is by (shameless plug) [following me](https://twitter.com/#!/genetics_blog) or the [people I follow](http://twitter.com/#!/genetics_blog/following) (note: this isn't an endorsement of anyone on this list, and there are a few off-topic people I follow for my non-work interests). I can't possibly list everyone, but a few folks who tweet consistently on-topic and interesting content are: [Daniel MacArthur](https://twitter.com/#!/dgmacarthur), [Jason Moore](https://twitter.com/#!/moorejh), [Dan Vorhaus](https://twitter.com/#!/genomicslawyer), [23andMe](https://twitter.com/#!/23andMe), [OpenHelix](https://twitter.com/#!/OpenHelix), [Larry Parnell](https://twitter.com/#!/larry_parnell),[Francis Ouellette](https://twitter.com/#!/bffo), [Leonid Kruglyak](https://twitter.com/#!/leonidkruglyak), [Sean Davis](https://twitter.com/#!/seandavis12), [Joe Pickrell](https://twitter.com/#!/joe_pickrell), [The Galaxy Project](https://twitter.com/#!/galaxyproject), [J. Chris Pires](https://twitter.com/#!/JChrisPires), [Nick Loman](https://twitter.com/#!/pathogenomenick), and [Andrew Severin](https://twitter.com/#!/isugif). Also, a hashtag in twitter (prefixed by the \#), is used to mark keywords or topics in Twitter. I occasionally browse through the [\#bioinformatics](https://twitter.com/#!/search/%23bioinformatics) and [\#Rstats](https://twitter.com/#!/search/%23Rstats) hashtag.
